Reading Response #2

How to think differently about doing good as a creative person
Omayeli Arenyeka, 2018 - The Creative Independent

"Creative Savior Complex works in similar ways: without proper consideration, many of the ways we try to help as creatives may mean valuable resources that could've done a lot of good are wasted, or, at its worst, it can lead to people getting hurt."
  1. When should our creativity actually be used to “do good”, rather than take other more practical actionable avenues?
  2. If you do decide to get involved - de-centralize yourself!
  3. “Doing some good” is not an objective for a social impact project - it needs to be more specific. What are you gaining? Why are you doing it? How are you helping others?
"Your work does not have to be a solution to a problem, but consequently, you can't frame it as such if it isn't. You have to be honest about your intentions and your output, especially if people's lives are involved."

Pedro asked at the beginning of class “What is design” and I wasn't able to clearly answer that question. It felt like whatever answer I might have had overlapped with art which doesn't quite align with what I think of as "design".

  1. I think it is a response to a prompt or a set of guidelines
  2. Some people said it solved problems - this answer didn't quite sit well with me. I couldn't quite put it to words then, but after reading this article I have a better idea - it seemed like design is often thought of as creative problem solving to improve people's lives, but in order to have the “solution”, you, the creator, are impeded by the issue or that you have overcome the problem. (Of course, no one can stop you from helping if you haven't experienced the struggle before)
  3. Perhaps on paper, that doesn't seem like a problem, but more often than not, the individual users are facing more complex and nuanced challenges, and designing a “one-size-fits-all” solution is pigeonholing people into confirming to a standard. That doesn't solve the problem but rather categorizes the people into a subgroup. (Reference What Can A Body Do)

So… “Are you equipped to address the problem?” If you are creating to help other people - you have to also verify whether what you created helped. So if it didn't, what then?

Reading Response #3

Design thinking was supposed to fix the world. Where did it go wrong?
Article by Rebecca Ackermann, 2023 - MIT Technology Review


“Design Thinking” was packaged and sold as a formulaic solution to solve all problems - Didn't really leave room for ambiguity or nuance.

"Design thinking agencies usually didn't take on this last step themselves (implementation); consultants often delivered a set of “recommendations” to the organizations that hired them."

  1. The key to success for a design thinking generated solution is actually resources for implementation. Before implementation, your result is just an idea or a call to action, which can also be successful, but is not a practical solution.
  2. And a “successful” end of the design thinking process requires review post implementation for proper evaluation.
  3. Human-centered design is mission and objective driven. It seeks to elevate the people at the center of the issue or the people actually trying to resolve the issue. Anything else is, as George Aye argues, profit-centered design. You're coming in, getting some good PR material, moving onto the next project. (This is what capitalism feels like though)
  4. “Who have you talked to? Have you tried it out before we spend all this money? And then how are you telling the story of the work?”

"We are all creatives, design thinking promised, and we can solve any problem if we empathize hard enough."

I think the operative word here in this sentence is “empathize”. But do the executives have the bandwidth or proper training to conduct such work?

"Design thinking offered a structure for those cross-­disciplinary conversations and a way to articulate design's value within them.

Maybe design thinking was meant as a tool to facilitate conversations between various expertise rather than a silo of designers.

"Instead of “empathy,” “make” and “care” are the concepts that program leaders hope will shape the design education across all offerings.

“Care” pivots the centralization to those affected by the solution. The true stakeholders of the design solution.
