Project concepts

the thoughts behind the inception of YHONVM

what is it?

You Have One New Voice Message is an interactive installation that holds you in the threshold between longing and connection.
The space that a voice message occupies when a call goes unanswered, when connection falls just short of reach.
On a landline telephone, you are invited to call, to listen, and to leave your own words behind.
To reach towards something that was lost: a relationship, an identity, a time, or a place.

Through the receiver, you are given a choice: to listen or to speak.
To listen is to receive a stranger’s vulnerability, their words recorded before you arrived.
To speak is to offer a personal story, a realization, a goodbye, and trusting someone else will come after and hold it for you.

The same spot, the same phone; a different time, a different voice.

Across these accumulated voices, you sit with what it means to be witnessed by someone you may never know.
What lingers in another’s voice is not just the words left behind,
but the relief of knowing that grief, longing, and the ache of distance
are never entirely yours alone to carry.

Here, you are invited to hold space for a stranger’s words, and trust the silence enough to offer your own.

why did it?

Starting from 5 years old, my life has moved through a rhythm of goodbyes and reconnections.

Bouncing between parents while they shared custody of my sister and me.
Moving to Taiwan with mom, and returning to the US each summer for brief summer vacations to be with baba(爸爸) and ayi(阿姨).
Attending boarding school from 7th-9th grade, and going home on weekends.
Moving back to California for high school, and making the decision to stay for good.
More recently, relocating from San Francisco to New York City.

Each transition refines my understanding of longing, the space between separation and connection.
This work grows from those experiences,
translating my personal navigation of distance and belonging into an a communal experience.

Throughout each transition, there was always a way to bridge the lost connections.
For the most part, it involved a telephone: a landline, a payphone, a cell phone.
my entire childhood, I shared a bedroom with my sister.

In our Taiwan bedroom, we had a Panasonic cordless landline on which our family in the US could reach us. It was private for my sister and me. No one else in the house could use it. It was a mode of communication, but also functioned as a notification system that we had missed a connection whenever we saw the missed call light/voice message indicator lit.

This then continued into my middle school years, when I attended a boarding school in Taipei.

The school did not allow personal technology, so the only way to reach my mom was to call her on a payphone with an IC telephone card. I remember waiting in the line for the phone just to hear my mom’s voice for a few minutes, and the moment I heard my mom’s “hello”, I would be filled with intense emotions.

Telephones have always been a symbol of connection and disconnection throughout my youth. As a communication tool, and as a visual representation connection. I’d like to distill the interactions with this technology down to the basics - calling, waiting, and speaking aloud to be heard.
